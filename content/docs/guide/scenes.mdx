A **scene** is what a frame shows: a graph of connected nodes that runs on the device. Scenes are
built in a visual editor, then deployed onto the frame, where it runs.

<Callout title="Self-hosted backend, FrameOS Cloud and standalone frames">
  The same editor ships in the [self-hosted backend](/guide/backend), in
  [FrameOS Cloud](/guide/cloud), and on a [standalone frame's](/guide/standalone) own admin page.
  Everything here works in all three, except [compiled scenes](#execution), which need the
  self-hosted backend.
</Callout>

## Create a scene

Click **+ Add new scene**, give it a name, and open the editor.

![Creating a new scene](/images/app/new-scene.gif)

## The four node colors

- 🟥 **Red - events.** Entry points that trigger execution. The most important one is `render`,
  which fires at a set interval (every 60 seconds, every hour, whatever you choose). Others react
  to [buttons, touch and keyboards](#input), schedules, or custom events.
- 🟦 **Blue - render apps.** Draw things: images, text, calendars, gradients, split layouts.
- 🟩 **Green - data apps.** Fetch and transform data: download an image, query Home Assistant,
  parse an iCal feed, generate an image with OpenAI, take a screenshot of a web page.
- 🟨 **Yellow - state fields.** User-editable values (text, numbers, options) that can be changed
  live from the control page without redeploying.

Drag from the `render` event's connector to add a blue **Render image** app:

![Adding a render app](/images/app/edit-add-render.gif)

Then drag its **image** field out and pick a green data app to feed it - use **FrameOS Gallery**
if unsure. Press deploy:

![Adding a data app](/images/app/edit-add-data.gif)

## Caching

Every data app has cache controls. A scene might re-render every minute while its data app only
fetches a new image every 15 minutes - the cached value is reused in between. And if a re-render
produces the same image as before, an e-ink display won't refresh at all: no unnecessary flashing.

![Cache controls](/images/app/caching.gif)

## State fields

Add a yellow **state field** under the scene's "State" tab, then drag it onto the canvas and wire
it into any app input. Deploy once, and from then on you can change the value live - from the
frame's control page, the backend, or the [HTTP API](/guide/api#events).

![Adding a state field](/images/app/edit-add-text.gif)

![Editing state live](/images/app/edit-set-message.gif)

Fields are typed, and the type decides what the control page shows: text, long text, numbers,
booleans, a color picker, a date picker, a select box, raw JSON, a font picker, an image, a
scene picker - or a **path**, which is a file and folder browser over the frame's own
[assets](/guide/settings#assets). "Which photo folder should this play?" becomes a picker instead
of a typed-in string. A select's options are one per line, either a plain value or `value | Label`
when what the frame stores and what people read should differ. Write a literal pipe as `\|`.

A scene with both a latitude and a longitude field (`latitude` or `lat`, and `longitude`, `lon`,
`lng` or `long`) gets a **Use my location** button wherever its state is edited: the scene's
drawer, previews, schedule entries and split screen panels. It fills in both from your browser. A
page opened over plain HTTP, like a frame's own admin page on your LAN, gets no location from any
browser, and the button says so.

Every scene also has one field you don't add yourself: **Refresh interval (seconds)**, listed last
wherever the scene's fields show up - the control page, the backend, the cloud, schedules and
previews. It starts at the interval from the scene's settings - 300 seconds for a new scene - and
it is the only interval there is: a frame has no refresh interval of its own and renders on its
scenes'. Change it like any other field and the next sleep follows; a `0` or nothing, set from
outside (a schedule entry, the control form), means the scene's own interval. To decide where it sits, what it's called, or to hide
it (`access: private`), add your own numeric field and switch on **Use as refresh interval**. In
code it is `state.refreshInterval`. The **Next sleep duration** app is still a one-time override:
it sets how long to sleep after *this* render, and the render after that is back on the interval.

Two more switches give a field a job of its own. **Use as background** on a color field makes it
the scene's background - what shows around a picture that doesn't fill the screen, like the bars
beside a portrait photo - so people pick it on the frame's page; the *SD card image* and photo
album scenes have one. **Use as full screen** on a boolean field lets people
[ask the layout for the whole screen](#full-screen) from the scene's settings.

## Icon, category and other scene settings [#icon]

A scene's settings say how it shows up in lists and what it's good for:

- **Icon** - a small picture of the scene, drawn wherever a list shows it without its picture: the
  frame's scene switcher, the desktops' menus and windows, the scene tiles, the library and the
  [scene store](/guide/scene-store). It's one SVG path in a 24×24 box, filled with the color of
  the text around it, so it works on e-paper and in every theme. **Upload SVG** turns an `.svg`
  of filled shapes into that path (strokes and text don't convert), or paste a path in: a solid
  icon from Material Design Icons fits as it is. Every sample scene has one.
- **Category** - one of the store's: *Photos*, *Art & galleries*, *Clocks & time*, *Calendars &
  agendas*, *Weather*, *Home & dashboards*, *Games*, *Fun & animations*, *Notes & tools*, *AI &
  generative*, *Demos & examples* or *System*. The scene keeps the name as text (`"category": "Photos"`).
  The desktops and the scene switcher group their lists of scenes by it, *System* first, and the
  store files a published scene under it. *System* is for the frame's own apps - the Scene store,
  Code editor, Files, Terminal, Fonts, PDF viewer, Calculator, Books and Browser - and only a scene
  that names it is filed there. A layout is filed by its kind instead: *Multi app layouts* for a
  desktop, *Device layouts* for one made for a particular frame, *Single app layouts* for the rest.
- **Window size** - the size the scene opens at in a window of a [desktop](#windows): square for
  a music player, tall for a to-do list, 800×480 when it's not set. It changes nothing outside a
  desktop.
- **Full screen** - the scene wants no layout around it ([full screen](#full-screen)).
- **Round screens** - whether the scene reads well in a circle ([round screens](#round-screens)).
- **Opens files** - the kinds of file the scene reads, by extension: `pdf`, `epub`,
  `txt md json`. The *Files* scene sends a file that isn't a picture to the first scene that names
  its kind, with the file in the scene's `file` field, and says *Can't open .mp3 files* when none
  does. The *PDF viewer*, *Books* and *Code editor* samples name theirs.

## Scenes inside scenes

A **split** (or any scene node) draws other scenes into cells, and each of them renders on its own
schedule: a clock that ticks every second next to a photo that changes every
hour re-runs only the clock, and the photo's cell puts back what it drew last time. The split's
own interval is the fastest it renders, so set it to the quick cell's pace; each cell's options
say when that cell renders. A render asked for from outside - a button, the API, the schedule -
redraws every cell.

In a [JSX app](/guide/apps#jsx-uis), `<Scene id="weather" />` does the same inside a UI: another
scene of the frame drawn into a box, on its own schedule, with the tag's props as its state, and
taps on it reach it in its own coordinates. Up to eight fit in one UI, and the scene draws straight
into its part of the canvas - a dashboard of scene tiles redraws only the tile that's due.

## Layouts [#layouts]

A **layout** is a scene that wraps your other scenes: a taskbar with the clock and the battery, a
newspaper masthead, a Polaroid border.

![The Windows 98 layout: a gray taskbar with Start, the scene's button, Control Panel and a clock, around a scene](/images/scenes/samples/windows-98-layout.jpg)

![The Front page layout: a newspaper masthead with the date and the scene's name above the scene](/images/scenes/samples/front-page-layout.jpg)

It's one [JSX app](/guide/apps#jsx-uis) with a `<yield />` where the scene goes:

```tsx title="app.tsx"
export function get(app: FrameOSApp) {
  const now = new Date()
  frameos.setNextSleep(60 - now.getSeconds()) // nothing to redraw until the minute changes
  return (
    <div className="flex flex-col bg-slate-900 text-white">
      <yield />
      <div className="flex justify-between px-4 py-2 text-xl">
        <span onClick={() => frameos.dispatch('sceneSwitcher')}>{app.frame.sceneName}</span>
        <span>{now.toTimeString().slice(0, 5)}</span>
      </div>
    </div>
  )
}
```

The scene draws straight into the hole and keeps its own schedule, and the chrome around it redraws
only when it's due, so a 20 fps animation inside a clock bar pays for the bar only when the clock
changes. Taps go to whatever is under them. A layout's app can read `app.frame.sceneName`, `app.frame.batteryPercent` and
`app.frame.wifiRssi`, and open the frame's own screens with `frameos.dispatch('sceneSwitcher')`,
`frameos.dispatch('sceneSettings')` and `frameos.dispatch('sceneSettings', { page: 'frame' })`.

- **Starters**: the [desktops](#windows), the device layouts and the full screen layouts - *Top bar*, *Bottom bar*,
  *Side rail*, *Polaroid*, *Front page*, *Terminal*, *Gallery wall*, *Teletext* and *E-reader* -
  are one click away under **New layout** in the frame's Layouts group, the two kinds listed
  apart, as they are in every layout select. *Teletext* gives each scene a page number, a clock
  and colored links to the scenes before and after; *E-reader* puts the scene on a page, with a
  footer whose halves turn to the scene before and after. *Terminal* has **F2 settings** at the
  right of its prompt: a tap or the F2 key opens the settings of the scene it wraps. The
  [scene store](/guide/scene-store) has a shelf for each kind.
- **Made for a reTerminal**: *reTerminal* (the E1001 to E1004) knows where each key sits on the
  case and writes what it does beside it - a press in large letters, a hold in small ones - and
  adds a status bar along the keys' edge with the signal, the battery and the time the picture was
  drawn. *reTerminal Sticky* turns the scene list into a home screen with the time and the date
  large, the signal and the battery, and a line each for what the Action key, Up and Down and the
  swipes do; held sideways, they go in a narrow column beside the list. Over every other scene it's a thin bar - the time on the left (a tap opens the scene
  list), the signal and the battery on the right (a tap opens the frame's page) - or nothing at
  all. Both follow the frame's [button actions](/guide/settings#buttons) and rotation, have
  switches for everything they show, and step aside while the keyboard is up.
- **Any scene can be one**: **Use as layout** in its settings turns it into a layout and back, if
  its render is one JSX app with a `<yield />` - the switch says why when it isn't. A layout stays
  out of the scene list, the switcher and next/previous, and a frame never boots to one.
- **Wearing one**: **Activate** in a layout's drawer puts it on every scene and pushes it, and
  **Deactivate** takes it off. A scene added later wears it too. Each scene can pick its own under
  **Advanced** in the frame's [Layout setting](/guide/settings#layout), and the frame's on-screen
  settings can change it on the device.
- **Around the built-in screens**: the scene switcher and the settings open inside the layout, so
  a taskbar stays up. A layout that wants them full-screen returns a bare `<yield />` while
  `app.frame.systemScene` is true.
- **Safe mode**: a layout can make a frame hard to use. Ten pulls down from the top edge of a touch
  screen, each within five seconds of the last, open the frame's settings without the layout:
  **Cancel** goes back as it was, and only **Save** changes anything. A swipe in from another edge
  in between starts the count again.
- **Its look on the frame's own screens**: the scene settings, the frame page, the switcher and the
  keyboard on the screen take their look from the layout around them. The desktops below have a
  **Look of the settings pages** field - Windows Standard, Light and Dark, BeOS, Classic,
  NeXTSTEP, Warp 4, Commander, High contrast, or FrameOS's own (Omarchy's pages wear its theme) -
  and e-paper gets High contrast. A
  JavaScript app can wear the same look ([`look`](/guide/apps#jsx-uis)), as the *Files* sample does.

### A scene that wants the whole screen [#full-screen]

A reader or a game can ask the layout to step aside: switch on **Full screen** in the scene's
settings, or give it a public boolean field with **Use as full screen**, so people turn it on and
off from the scene's settings on the frame. The *reTerminal Sticky* layout then gives the scene the
whole glass, and *Top bar*, *Bottom bar*, *Side rail* and *E-reader* do the same; the desktops and
the picture-frame layouts keep their look. A change shows on the next render, with no restart.
*Books* and *PDF viewer* have the field, on by default. In your own layout,
`app.frame.fullScreen` is true while the scene asks, and a bare `<yield />` gives it the screen.

### Desktops: scenes side by side [#windows]

On a Raspberry Pi, an ESP32 and in the browser preview, nine layouts are desktops: *Windows 98*,
*macOS*, *BeOS*, *Classic Mac*, *NeXTSTEP*, *OS/2 Warp*, *Tiling*, *Omarchy* and *Commander*. Every
scene you open gets a window, a tile or a panel of its own, and they all run at once. The one in
front is the frame's current scene - the one the scene list, next and previous, the API and the
control planes move. Each has a **Windows** setting: *On*, *Off* (one scene at a time, with the
desktop's bar around it) or *Automatic*, which runs windows where the frame can run four scenes at
once or has no limit and has a screen of at least 640×480, and one scene elsewhere.

- **Windows 98**, **macOS**, **BeOS**, **Classic Mac** and **NeXTSTEP** open overlapping windows,
  moved and sized with a finger or the mouse, snapped to half or all of the desktop at its edges,
  put away and brought back, each in its own chrome: a taskbar and Start menu, a Dock and menu bar,
  a Deskbar and Be menu, System 7's pinstriped title bars in black and white, NeXTSTEP's main menu,
  Dock and miniwindows in four greys. Alt+Tab, F11 for full screen, and a gear on each window for
  that scene's settings. With a mouse on a Pi, the cursor turns into resize arrows over a
  window's edges.
- **Icons** on the desktop of *Windows 98*, *BeOS*, *Classic Mac* and *OS/2 Warp* move: drag on
  bare desktop for a dotted rectangle that picks every icon it touches (Ctrl or Shift adds to what's
  picked), and drag a picked icon to move them all. Each lands in the free spot nearest where it was
  dropped and stays there across restarts, and new windows keep clear of them.
- **OS/2 Warp** has the WarpCenter along the top (its Desktop menu with the scenes by category,
  the Window List, the tray, the clock), Warp 4's grey windows with a system menu behind the title
  icon, and the LaunchPad at the bottom of the desktop. A right click or a long press on bare
  desktop opens its pop-up menu, and OS/2's keys work: Ctrl+Esc for the Window List, Alt+F9 to
  hide, Alt+F10 to maximize, Alt+F4 to close.
- **A new window** opens at its scene's own [window size](#icon), or 800×480. One too big for the
  desktop fills it when it's the first, and is made smaller, keeping its shape, when it isn't. It
  goes where it covers no other window if there's room, and where it covers the least if not, so
  it never lands right on top of another. The zoom or maximize button, or letting go at the top
  edge, fills the desktop, and the scene opens that way again until the frame restarts.
- **Tiling** keeps the scenes in tiles, in five arrangements its top bar steps through. Drag a gap
  to move that split, drag a tile's bar onto another tile to swap them, and a press anywhere in a
  tile is its scene's.
- **Omarchy** tiles its scenes with gaps and a border over five workspaces, after Omarchy 4: a
  bar along the top with the workspaces, the layout, the clock, the signal and the battery, and a
  layout per workspace - dwindle, scrolling columns, or floating windows with a dock. Super+Space
  opens a menu that filters as you type, and Omarchy's bindings work (Super+1 to 0, Super+W,
  Super+F, Super+arrows, Super+Return for the terminal, Super+K lists them all). The **Super key**
  setting says which key is Super: *Super or Ctrl* by default, so the bindings work in a browser,
  which keeps Cmd+W for itself. Eleven of Omarchy's themes, Tokyo Night first.
- **Commander** is two blue panels: the scene in front in one and a second scene running in the
  other. Tab or a press moves between them, and the function keys along the bottom swap the
  panels, show the next scene, turn the other panel into an Info panel or give one panel the whole
  screen.
- **Lists of scenes** - the Start menu, the Scenes menus, the Be menu, the Desktop menu, Tiling's + - go by
  [category](#icon), *System* first, a heading over each and the scenes without one last under *Other*, and scroll
  under a finger when they're longer than the screen. Each scene shows its own [icon](#icon) where
  it has one. The macOS Dock has the scenes with a window open, NeXTSTEP's the ones that have run.
- **The frame's own screens are windows too.** Each scene's settings open as a window beside it,
  and **Save** there applies the values to the running scene and keeps the window open. The
  scene list is a launcher: a scene picked there opens beside it, and the list stays open until
  you close it. Closing a window opens nothing else - the focus goes to the window under it - and
  closing a scene closes its settings window too. The keyboard on the screen is a window of its
  own over all the others, with a bar to drag it by.
- **Memory.** On an ESP32 only so many scenes run at once, by the memory its panel leaves: two on
  an 8&nbsp;MB LCD or 7.5" board, one on the 13.3" E1004, four with 16&nbsp;MB or more. The
  desktop closes the window that's been in the background longest, and a window behind another
  runs at most every five seconds until it's brought forward. A Pi and the browser have no limit.
  A scene brought forward from its window doesn't reload.
- **Another size.** A new render scale, a turn of the frame or a resized browser window puts every
  window at the same place on the new desktop, in proportion.

Pick another desktop and it opens the windows the last one had open, where they were on the
screen. In your own layout, `app.frame.scenes` lists the frame's scenes (only where they can run
side by side) with each one's window size, icon and category,
`app.frame.sceneId` is the one in front, `app.frame.windows` how many may run at once, and a bare
`<Scene id="…" />` is that scene itself, state and all, not a copy. Where scenes can't run side by
side - a [board rendered on the server](/guide/esp32#render-on-server), a virtual frame - a
desktop draws its bar around the one scene.

## Buttons, touch and keyboards [#input]

A frame's GPIO buttons reach a scene as two events:

- **`button`** - what the person meant: a `press`, delivered the moment the key comes up, or a
  `longPress`, delivered once when the key has been held a second, while it's still down - a frame
  with a buzzer sounds then, so you know to let go. Every press is exactly one `button` event, so a
  `button` listener without an action filter never runs twice for one press, and two quick presses
  are two presses. A key that a rule of the frame's or the scene's
  [button actions](/guide/settings#buttons) binds a double or triple press of also has a
  `doublePress` or `triplePress`; only such a key waits to see whether another press comes, so
  every other key stays as quick. Each press carries the key's `role` - `primary`, `next`, `prev`,
  `back`, `menu` or `screen` - so a scene that listens by role works on every board.
- **`gpio`** - what the line did: `down` and `up`, the moment they happen. Listen for these to
  count every tap, or for hold-to-show.

Each edge is timestamped when the frame sees it, so a tap made while an e-ink panel spends 30
seconds refreshing is still a tap, and two of them are still two presses. In the event node,
the label and pin filters pick from the frame's own buttons.

On a Pi with a touchscreen, a mouse or a keyboard, scenes also hear
`pointerDown`, `pointerMove` and `pointerUp` (one pointer per finger), `wheel`, `keyDown` and
`keyUp` with the key in the frame's keyboard layout, `textInput`, and the gestures `tap`,
`doubleTap`, `longPress`, `swipe` and `pinch` (two fingers that moved, as one event when they
lift, with the `scale` they ended at) - all in the scene's own pixels, rotated frames included.
The older `mouseMove` / `mouseDown` / `mouseUp` names keep working. The *Touch test* and
*Keyboard test* sample scenes show every event as it arrives, and the Touch test draws a drag's
path. The browser preview sends the same events from your mouse and keyboard, two fingers on a
touch screen reach the scene as a pinch instead of zooming the page, and a preview button you hold
down is held like a frame's key. A `pointerDown` or `tap` says which of Shift, Ctrl, Alt and Meta
were held. A mouse on a Pi gets a cursor of its own, drawn over the picture and never into it,
whose shape follows what's under it: a hand on a link or a button, an I-beam over a field, the
`cursor-*` classes of a [JSX UI](/guide/apps#jsx-uis).
The touch screens of [ESP32 boards](/guide/esp32#lcd) send the same
pointer events and gestures. A touch `longPress` is held half a second, as on a phone; a
button's is a second. A swipe that starts at the very edge of a touch screen is the frame's (`edgeSwipe`) and
never reaches the scene.

**The keys.** Every board's keys follow one standard
([every board's keys](/guide/buttons)): a short press belongs to the scene and a hold
to the frame. **Action** presses what's highlighted. **Next** and **Prev** walk what there is to
press, and on a scene with nothing to press they're its `forward` and `back`; held, they switch to
the next or previous scene. **Back** is `back`, and held it's Home. **Home** opens the scene
switcher, and held, the scene's settings. **Screen** turns an LCD off and on. A board with no Back
key gets one from two keys pressed together. Keys that point somewhere follow the frame's rotation,
so Next is towards the right or the bottom of the picture however the frame hangs. A key pressed
or an edge swiped on a dark screen only turns it on, as a touch does.

**Swipes in from the edge.** On every HyperPixel and every ESP32 touch board - the SenseCAP
Indicator, the Sunton and Guition LCDs, Elecrow's CrowPanel LCDs, the round AMOLED, the reTerminal
E1003 and Sticky - the
edges come bound to the frame:

| Swipe | What it does |
| --- | --- |
| Up from the bottom | The **scene switcher**: every scene on the frame as a tile, in the frame's order. Tap one to show it, or the gear in its header for the settings of the scene you came from, whose own gear is the [frame's page](/guide/settings#on-screen). |
| Down from the top | The **scene settings**: the public fields of the scene showing, as a form, with a keyboard on the screen. **Save** applies them; **Cancel** or the same swipe goes back as it was. The gear left of **Save** opens the [frame's own settings](/guide/settings#on-screen). |
| In from the left | `back`, delivered to the scene: the photo or the page before. |
| In from the right | `forward`, the other way. |

`back` and `forward` never change the scene, and a scene that doesn't listen for them stays as it
is. Changing the scene on a touch screen is the switcher's job. In the switcher they move the
highlight a screenful, and in the settings `back` is Cancel. While a field has the keyboard up,
`back` puts the keyboard away (or closes an open list) and goes no further. Both screens close themselves if left alone: the switcher
after 60 seconds (five minutes on e-paper), the settings after five minutes. A press on the
on-screen keyboard is always a key, even on the outer keys at the edge of the glass, and a swipe up
from the bottom over the keyboard still opens the switcher.

The switcher is a folder's icon view, as Explorer and Finder show one: each scene's
[icon](#icon) over its name, grouped by [category](#icon) with *System* first, in one list that
scrolls, opened on the row of the scene you came from. Its icons keep one size, so a bigger
screen shows more of them, not bigger ones. Where it's wider than tall - a frame held sideways,
the 5.79" strip - each name sits beside its icon, so more scenes fit, and on a round panel the
columns sit in the middle of the circle. A search field in its header keeps the scenes whose
name, category or id hold what you type, the first one lit for Enter. On a desktop it's the
desktop's window of every scene, in the desktop's look. A touch frame that was given no scene to
start on opens on the switcher as its home: there it has no Close and never times out, and a scene
picked there is the one the frame boots into next.
The switcher works without touch too. On e-paper it's black on white and the keys walk a highlight:
`next` and `prev` step, `primary` and `refresh` show the scene (the reTerminal's green key is
`primary`), `back` and `menu` go back. Past the
last tile the highlight reaches the gear, which opens the scene's settings and from there the
frame's, so even a layout that broke every scene can be taken off from the switcher. Every frame
with keys reaches it: the Home key, or two keys held together on a board that has none
([every board](/guide/buttons#every-board)). Or send `sceneSwitcher`
[over HTTP](/guide/api#events).

**Whose buttons?** A scene listens for the keys its author had - `A` and `B`, or a `next` role -
and your frame has the keys it has. Two maps sit between them:

- **Map buttons**, in an installed scene's **…** menu, lists the scene's button listeners and lets
  you pick which press of which of your frame's buttons each one answers to - REFRESH is this
  scene's `A`, a long press of REFRESH its `B`. It belongs to the scene as installed on this frame:
  the scene itself is untouched, and an update to a newer version keeps the mapping.
- **Button actions**, in the frame's [settings](/guide/settings#buttons), bind a press or an edge
  swipe to the frame itself - next scene, previous scene, the scene switcher, render - whatever
  scene is showing, and the scene never hears it.

A `setCurrentScene` event can name a `direction` (`next` or `prev`) instead of a scene, to walk the
frame's scene list.

In a [JSX UI](/guide/apps#jsx-uis), `next` and `prev` walk the elements there are to press; in a
box that scrolls they turn its page once the next element isn't in sight, so a long list or a long
text pages through on a frame with keys alone.

On an LCD - an HDMI screen, a HyperPixel, the SenseCAP Indicator - any render that hasn't put its
picture up within 200 ms shows three pulsing dots over the old picture until it does: a scene
switch, a slow tap, a slow refresh. A quick one looks as it always did. On the Indicator a render
that answers a tap, a swipe or a key waits 500 ms before showing them, animations included, so a
UI that answers in a few hundred milliseconds never flashes them.

## Round screens [#round-screens]

On a round panel - the HyperPixel 2.1" Round, Waveshare's
[ESP32-S3-Touch-AMOLED-1.75](/devices/waveshare-esp32-s3-touch-amoled-1in75), Elecrow's
[CrowPanel 1.46" rotary](/devices/elecrow-crowpanel-1in46-rotary) - only the circle
inside the square shows, and the frame's own screens keep inside it: the scene switcher is a lens,
its tiles full size in the middle and smaller towards the rim; the scene settings and the frame
page are a round form, the name in the top arc with Back and Save under it; a list's scroll bar
runs along the rim; the keyboard, the edge swipes and an error's message stay in the circle. The
frame's own apps - the Scene store, Files, Terminal, Code editor - do too, and most samples lay
out for the circle: clocks as dials, progress as rings along the rim, games in the square inside
the circle with their controls in the arcs. Text from the *Text* app drawn on the whole canvas keeps
to the circle too.

In your own scene, `context.round` (in an app or a code node) is true while it draws a round
panel's whole canvas, and false in a window, a split's cell or a layout's hole, so a scene can keep
inside the circle where it is one and change nothing anywhere else; `app.frame.round` says the
panel is round. **Round screens** in the scene's settings says whether it reads well in a circle,
and code that asks either counts as saying yes. The [scene store](/guide/scene-store) shows such
scenes with a **Round screen** tag and filters by it, and a round frame's store lists them first.

## Video [#video]

A frame on a Raspberry Pi plays video: a folder of files left playing for ever, a camera, a
stream, old films. The samples are *Video player* - played like a phone's: a tap for the controls,
a bar to drag, a double tap on either side to skip ten seconds, a swipe for the next video, and
the keys too - *Immich videos*, *Camera wall*, *Video from URL*, *Archive films* and *Slow movie*
(a film as e-paper stills), and *Files* plays the videos it lists. They need a Pi: the store
doesn't offer them to an ESP32, and a backend installs ffmpeg on Raspberry Pi OS when a scene
needs it.

ffmpeg decodes each video at the size of the box it's drawn in, and only that box is repainted.
On a Pi 0 to 4 an H.264 video goes through the VideoCore's own decoder, and on a HyperPixel a
video nothing covers is shown on a display plane of its own, which the display scales as it scans
out - no pixel of it passes through the CPU, so a Pi Zero W plays 720p and 1080p clips in real
time, a phone's upright video included. On HDMI, a turned panel or under a window it plays in the
canvas, slower. Each playing video logs a `video:playback` line every 30 seconds with its frame
rate and its late and dropped frames. In a JavaScript app it's [`frameos.video()`](/guide/apps#video).

## The store, your files, a terminal and a code editor, on the frame [#frame-apps]

These sample scenes work the frame itself from its own screen. Install them like any other scene;
the [desktops](#windows) give the store, Files, the terminal and the code editor an entry of
their own.

- **Scene store** browses the [scene store](/guide/scene-store) on the glass: *Scenes* and
  *Layouts* as tabs, the categories down the left of a wide window (a narrow one lists them first
  and opens one in a step of its own), search, a **Back** that says where it goes, and **Preview**,
  **Install**, **Open**, **Update** and **Delete**. On a fast screen a tile is the scene's cover
  with its icon as a badge, and a category is one grid that scrolls; e-paper keeps icons, a page a
  screen. A frame isn't offered scenes it can't run: e-paper and an ESP32 skip the ones that need
  a fast screen or a Pi, and an LCD puts the fast ones first. A layout can be tried: it goes around
  every scene, the store included, and **Keep** installs it while **Discard** puts back what the
  frame wore. The store reopens where you left it after a preview. An **Updates** entry with a
  count tops the categories when the store has newer versions of the frame's scenes, with
  **Update** on each and **Update all** in one push; a narrow window shows the categories as a
  grid of tiles.
  The frame asks its backend or the cloud to make the change, so only public store scenes go in,
  never one that would let a scene do more than the copy it replaces (that's the owner's call, from
  the workspace), and never while the [settings on the screen](/guide/settings#on-screen) are off.
  When the backend or the cloud doesn't answer, the store says *The control plane did not answer*. In the browser
  preview it previews and installs nothing. On a Pi and an ESP32 that stays awake the covers
  download in the background, so a tap never waits for one, and a scene's own icon stands in
  until its cover arrives.
- **Files** walks the frame's [files](/guide/settings#assets): folders and pictures as
  thumbnails or a list, a picture on the whole screen with a swipe to the next, a slideshow, and
  **New folder**. An ESP32 with a TF card and internal storage shows both as two places at the
  top. In the browser preview and the playground it has **Upload** and **Download**, for files on
  your computer. On a desktop it's drawn in that desktop's look. **Reload** lists the folder
  again, and a TF card that can't be read right now says so, with **Try again**, instead of
  looking empty. A file that isn't a picture opens in the scene that reads its kind
  ([Opens files](#icon)) - a PDF in the PDF viewer, an `.epub` in Books, text in the Code editor -
  and a video plays on a Pi. Making a folder is a write, so
  it wears the *reads & writes files* tag.
- **Terminal** is the ESP32 serial console's commands - `help`, `status`, `set`, `wifi`,
  `wifi-scan`, `render`, `event`, `scenes`, `scene`, `restart`, `reboot` - on the screen, with the
  keyboard on the screen and the last 200 lines kept. It can do what the frame's settings page can
  and nothing more, and wears a red *changes frame settings* tag ([permissions](/guide/apps#permissions)).
- **Code editor** shows every scene on the frame as files - `scene.json`, each app's files, each
  code node's code - in a workbench after VS Code: an explorer with the scenes by category, tabs,
  the code colored, wrapped (or not, from the status bar) and typed into with the keyboard on the
  screen or a real one. **Save** puts the change on the frame. **Ask AI** sends a request to the
  frame's backend or cloud, whose scene assistant writes a new scene or a change to the one open,
  shown as a draft to **Discard** or **Save & show**. A save never lets a scene do more than it was
  allowed. The scene settings' **Code** button opens it on that scene. **Open folder** opens a
  folder of the frame's files instead, as VS Code does - its tree in the explorer and any text file
  in the editor, saved back to the frame. It works only while the
  frame offers its settings page, and wears a red *reads and changes this frame's scenes* tag.
- **Books** reads the `.epub` books on the frame's card and internal storage: a library with
  covers, the book read last first, pages laid out to fit at any text size (two columns on a wide
  screen), pictures, the contents, **A-** / **A+**, and every book opened where you left it. Taps,
  swipes, the keys and back and forward turn the pages.
- **PDF viewer** does the same for `.pdf` files: a page fits the screen whole, or is read at the
  screen's width a part at a time when that would leave it too small. The PDF's bookmarks are its
  contents, and a digit opens a keypad to go to a page. Text is drawn in the frame's own face,
  stretched to where the PDF puts it; embedded fonts, encryption and JPEG 2000 or JBIG2 pictures
  aren't read. Both open full screen in a [layout that allows it](#full-screen).
- **Browser** is a text browser, the way Lynx and Opera Mini were: no scripts and no style sheets,
  the page in one column in the frame's own type and the colors its markup names, slimmed by the
  frame as it downloads and drawn a screen at a time, so even a 7&nbsp;MB news front page is on an
  ESP32's glass in seconds. The start page is a search (DuckDuckGo) and your bookmarks as tiles;
  there's history, an instant Back, forms, text size, pictures on or off, a link's QR code, a
  site's feed and a page's archived copy. Not in the playground.
- **Fonts** lists the frame's fonts a page at a time, each name in its own face, and a specimen of
  the one you pick. **Get fonts** downloads more straight onto the frame from a catalog of 205
  families, by kind or by search.

## How scenes run [#execution]

Scenes are **interpreted**: nothing about them is built into the frame's binary. When a scene
starts, the frame turns its graph into one small JavaScript program and runs that. New scenes are
created that way, every scene FrameOS ships is built that way, and it's the only way
[ESP32](/guide/esp32) and [Pico](/guide/pico) frames can work at all. What you get:

- **Deploys are near-instant** - nothing is recompiled, so a change is on the frame in seconds.
- **Code nodes are JavaScript, TypeScript or JSX**, and apps can be customised in JavaScript.
- It costs a little more per render, which matters very little on a display that takes 20 seconds
  to refresh anyway.
- **Scenes are kept apart.** A scene with code of its own runs in a JavaScript runtime of its own
  on a Pi and on an ESP32 with more than 8&nbsp;MB of PSRAM, so one that runs out of memory takes no
  other scene with it. On an 8&nbsp;MB board each gets a sandbox of its own in a shared runtime.

There is a second, older mode - **compiled** - which turns the scene into native code inside the
frame's binary. It is a **legacy** path: it's the only mode that can run custom **Nim** app source
and Nim code nodes, but it forces a FrameOS source build on every deploy, and it only works on a
Raspberry Pi deployed from a [self-hosted backend](/guide/backend). The cloud refuses compiled
scenes outright, at upload and at install. Nothing in the editor creates one any more: new code
nodes are always JavaScript, and saving or installing a Nim-only app asks before it flips a scene
to compiled. Compiled scenes wear an amber **legacy compiled** tag - on the frame list, in the
deploy drawer and in the scene sidebar.

The way out is the converter: **Convert to an interpreted scene**, in the scene's menu, in Scene
Settings and on any "Legacy Nim" node. It ports Nim code nodes and scene-local Nim apps to
JavaScript - simple expressions convert deterministically, the rest goes through an AI pass, and
whatever can't be ported is reported as needing a manual port. It converts the editor's unsaved
copy in place, so check the result, then save or deploy. The Nim does not survive the conversion -
export the scene first if you want a backup. A backend forwards the conversion to the cloud, using
your OpenAI key from **Settings → OpenAI** when one is set. The same converter is a public page at
[scenes.frameos.net/nim-converter](https://scenes.frameos.net/nim-converter) (paste scene JSON, no
login) and a `scene_convert` MCP tool.

<Callout title="Compiled scenes are the explicit exception">
  A scene is interpreted unless it says otherwise: templates, imports, backups and AI-built scenes
  that carry no execution mode all run interpreted. A scene that carries Nim without saying it is
  compiled stays interpreted too, and Scene Settings flags the Nim it can't run. The **Execution**
  select and the frame's *Installation mode* live under an **Advanced** disclosure on a Pi frame.
  Build new scenes interpreted unless you specifically need Nim. If you switch an existing scene
  between modes, do a full deploy afterwards; the editor warns you when a scene still contains Nim
  source that interpreted mode can't run.
</Callout>

On the frame side there are two ways a binary gets built: **precompiled** (the release binary plus
its driver libraries, scenes interpreted - the default) and **static** (everything compiled into
one binary, which is what a compiled scene forces). Frames still configured with the retired
`shared` or `shared-scenes` modes are moved to `static` automatically.

## Push, activate, preview

**Edit** on a scene - from its tile, its drawer, the frames home, the library or a chat link -
opens the scene's page: the same workspace as a [scene store](/guide/scene-store) page, with
**Editor**, **AI** and **Preview** as columns you toggle and resize. The editor is a file tree
(the scene, its apps and their files) beside the node graph, the AI edits the scene with the
frame as context, and the preview runs it in your browser. **Save** saves it, **Save & push** puts
it on the frame too. A saved scene on a self-hosted backend opens in the same workspace.

A frame's scene drawer has three buttons. The main one puts the scene on the frame as it stands,
saved or not: **Push & activate** (or **Push to frame** on the scene that's already active) while
the frame doesn't have your latest edits, plain **Activate** (or **Apply**) once it does. It pushes only that
scene and the scenes it draws or switches to - settings and your other unsaved edits stay where
they are. **Preview** runs the scene in your browser with the values in the drawer, and **Reset**
puts them back. Scenes are *pushed*; settings and FrameOS itself are *deployed*.

Installing a scene - from the store, the Scenes page, or dropped on a frame's tile - saves it and
pushes it to the frame without changing what's showing. **Install & activate**, in the install
dialog and on every catalog row, does both in one push.

A deploy from the frame's deploy drawer also moves installed store scenes to their newest
versions, on the cloud and on a backend. They're listed under **Scene updates**, ticked: untick
one to keep the version the frame has, or click it to see what changes - the scene and each app's
code as files, line by line. On a backend, a scene you edited keeps your field values.

Two ways to see a scene without committing to it:

- **Preview in browser** runs the scene *in your browser*. The frame's renderer is compiled to
  WebAssembly, so you get the real thing - same apps, same layout engine, same resolution -
  without deploying to a device, or before you own one. It comes with a **Browser assets** folder
  that lives only in your browser (seeded with sample photos, so slideshow and SD-card scenes
  render), JavaScript clocks in the frame's time zone, and a real-time toggle for scenes that want
  to run faster than one frame a second. A **Device** menu above Resolution puts the preview on a
  real panel - the Seeed reTerminal E1001 to E1004 and Sticky, the Waveshare PhotoPainter and 13.3",
  Inky Impressions and the Inky Frame, the CrowPanel, the SenseCAP Indicator, HyperPixels, the Pi
  touch displays, a 1080p or 4K screen - with that panel's size and inks. The two round panels are
  drawn round, and the scene is told so. **Rotate** turns it and
  keeps the device. A **Layout** select under it tries the scene inside any of your layouts or the
  starters, without changing the scene, and **Settings** beside it sets that layout's fields for
  the preview, kept in your browser. A layout you're editing runs around the frame's other
  scenes, and a desktop runs windowed. Copy and paste in the scene go through your computer's
  clipboard, a tapped link opens a tab, and a file the scene saves downloads - those two only after
  a click or a key on the preview itself, since a store scene's code is anyone's. A scene that runs out of the memory limit stops and
  says so, with a **Restart** button. On a self-hosted backend the preview fetches through the
  backend, so it reaches what the frame reaches, your LAN included, without CORS errors; a host
  it refuses says why.
- **Preview on frame** temporarily shows the scene on the actual frame without saving or
  deploying it. Good for checking how a color dithers on real e-ink. A sleeping ESP32 gets it on
  its next wake. While it's on the frame, the scene list shows it as a dashed *preview* row above
  your saved scenes, with **Install** to add it to the frame's scenes and **Discard** to take it
  off. Until you pick one, the preview stays: a deploy or another push
  carries it along. Pictures and scenes made by the [voice chat](/guide/esp32#voice-chat) arrive
  the same way.

## How to learn

FrameOS ships with many sample scenes. The fastest way to learn is to install them, open them in
the editor, and poke around. Besides photos and dashboards there are weather scenes on Open-Meteo
that need no key (a painted window, a newspaper column, a 24-hour dial, a year of weather, two days
as one long sky), clocks (flip, Nixie, a world clock, the sun and the moon, a countdown), calendars
(a week planner, an agenda in five looks, *Up next*, a year at a glance, and six looks for the
month), AI scenes that use your OpenAI key at most once a period (the news and the weather painted,
a story a chapter a day, a tarot card and a word of the day), games played by tap
and key (Sudoku, Solitaire, Mahjong, Minesweeper, 2048, Checkers, Reversi, Connect four and more,
with computer players that answer in under a second), real-time arcade games for fast screens in
the 8-bit palette mode (Snake, Brick breaker, Block drop, Moon lander, Star shooter, Road racer,
Cave flyer, Paddle duel - the turn-based ones turn into puzzles on e-paper), tools (Daily chores,
Pomodoro, Download to assets, Sketchbook and Handwriting notes, whose strokes keep every point the
finger passed, Pixel art), a Hacker News reader drawn like the site, and touch remotes for Home
Assistant, Homey and Spotify (signed in with your own Spotify app's Client ID). Every sample lays
out for the panel it's on - from a 240×296 watch to the 13.3" E1004, round glass and black-and-white
or Spectra e-paper included - and the games and tools can be used with a frame's keys alone: Next
walks what there is to press and the frame's Back leaves each page they open.
